Week 3

Pre-Reading Context

Naaman — Commander of the entire army of Syria (also called Aram). He was the second most powerful man in the nation, highly honored by his king, and a mighty warrior. Scripture says the LORD had given victory to Syria through him—some Jewish traditions identify him as the archer who killed King Ahab of Israel in battle. He had wealth, authority, and the respect of his nation. He also had leprosy—a skin disease that would eventually end his career, isolate him from society, and kill him. Everything he had accomplished could not cure him.

The Servant Girl — A young Israelite girl captured during one of Syria's raids into Israel. She had been torn from her home and family and made a slave in the household of the man who commanded the army that took her. Yet instead of bitterness, she told her mistress about the prophet in Israel who could heal her master. She had no power, no position, and no reason to help—but she spoke up anyway, and through her, God set the entire story in motion.

The King of Syria (Ben-Hadad II) — Naaman's master. He sent Naaman to Israel with a letter addressed to the king of Israel, along with enormous wealth: ten talents of silver (about 750 pounds), six thousand shekels of gold (about 150 pounds), and ten sets of clothing. He assumed the prophet would do whatever the king of Israel commanded, not understanding how God actually works.

The King of Israel (Jehoram) — Son of the wicked King Ahab and Queen Jezebel. When he received the letter asking him to cure Naaman's leprosy, he tore his robes in distress, assuming it was a pretext for war. He had no relationship with God and no thought of the prophet Elisha. His panic revealed how far Israel's leadership had drifted from the LORD.

Elisha — The prophet of God in Israel, successor to Elijah. When he heard that the king had torn his robes, he sent word: "Let him come to me, and he shall know that there is a prophet in Israel." Elisha would not be impressed by Naaman's position, wealth, or entourage. He would not even come to the door.

Naaman's Servants — The men who traveled with Naaman and witnessed his rage when Elisha's instructions offended him. They reasoned with him: "If the prophet had told you to do some great thing, would you not have done it? How much more, then, when he tells you, 'Wash and be cleansed'?" Their counsel brought him back from the edge of walking away unhealed.

Discussion Questions

  • Naaman was powerful and respected—but he had something destroying him slowly that he couldn't fix. What's the thing in your life that success can't touch?
  • Naaman had a whole picture in his head of how this was supposed to go. When has your expectation of how God should work gotten in the way of what He was actually doing?
  • Naaman would have done something hard. He resisted something simple. Why is humble obedience sometimes harder for men than difficult achievement?
  • It took his servants—people beneath him—to talk sense into him. Who in your life speaks truth to you that you're tempted to dismiss?
  • Naaman dipped seven times, not once. What does repeated, unglamorous obedience build that one dramatic moment doesn't?
