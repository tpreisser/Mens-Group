Week 5

Pre-Reading Context

Nehemiah — A Jew serving as cupbearer to the Persian King Artaxerxes. This was a position of extraordinary trust—Nehemiah tasted the king's wine to ensure it wasn't poisoned and served as a trusted advisor. When he heard that Jerusalem's walls lay in ruins and its people in disgrace, he wept, fasted, and prayed. Then he asked the king for permission to go rebuild. The king not only granted his request but provided letters of safe passage and materials for construction. Nehemiah arrived in Jerusalem as its governor, inspected the broken walls at night, and rallied the people: "Let us rise up and build."

Sanballat the Horonite — Governor of Samaria, the region north of Judah. He was likely from Beth-Horon and represented the Samaritans—a people who had mixed the worship of Yahweh with pagan practices and intermarried with foreign nations. From the moment Nehemiah arrived, Sanballat opposed him. A rebuilt Jerusalem threatened his political influence. He used mockery, threats, conspiracy, and false accusations to try to stop the work.

Tobiah the Ammonite — An official governing the region east of the Jordan River. The Ammonites were ancient enemies of Israel, descendants of Lot, known for worshiping Molech. Tobiah worked alongside Sanballat to discourage and intimidate the builders. He mocked the wall, saying even a fox climbing on it would break it down. He also had family connections among the Jewish nobles, which gave him informants inside Jerusalem.

Geshem the Arab — A leader controlling territory south of Judah. Together with Sanballat and Tobiah, he surrounded the Jews on three sides. These three men represented a coalition of enemies—political, ethnic, and religious—united in their opposition to Jerusalem's restoration.

The Builders — The priests, nobles, merchants, goldsmiths, perfume makers, and ordinary families of Jerusalem who worked side by side to rebuild the wall. They are listed by name in Nehemiah 3. Each family rebuilt the section nearest their own home. They would become both construction workers and soldiers.

Nearly 150 years earlier, Babylon had destroyed Jerusalem, torn down its walls, and carried the people into exile. The Persian Empire later conquered Babylon, and King Cyrus allowed the Jews to return. Some went back and rebuilt the Temple under Zerubbabel. Years later, Ezra led another group and restored the Law. But the walls of Jerusalem remained broken. Without walls, the city had no defense, no dignity, and no future.

Nehemiah arrived in 445 BC with royal authority and a burden from God. After surveying the ruins by night, he gathered the people and said, "You see the trouble we are in, how Jerusalem lies in ruins with its gates burned. Come, let us build the wall of Jerusalem, that we may no longer suffer derision." The people responded: "Let us rise up and build." And they began.

The enemies noticed immediately. Sanballat, Tobiah, and Geshem mocked them: "What are these feeble Jews doing? Will they restore it for themselves? Will they sacrifice? Will they finish up in a day? Will they revive the stones out of the heaps of rubbish, and burned ones at that?" Tobiah added: "If a fox goes up on what they are building, he will break down their stone wall."

Nehemiah prayed. The people kept building. The wall rose to half its height because "the people had a mind to work."

Then the opposition escalated. The enemies plotted to attack Jerusalem and create confusion. Nehemiah responded by arming the workers. From that point on, half the men worked while the other half stood guard with spears, shields, and bows. Those who carried materials held a load in one hand and a weapon in the other. Every builder worked with his sword strapped to his side. They did not change clothes. They slept in their work gear. A trumpet was ready to sound the alarm.

The wall continued to rise. Now the enemies tried a different approach.

Reading

When Sanballat heard that we were rebuilding the wall, he became angry and was greatly incensed. He ridiculed the Jews, and in the presence of his associates and the army of Samaria, he said, "What are those feeble Jews doing? Will they restore their wall? Will they offer sacrifices? Will they finish in a day? Can they bring the stones back to life from those heaps of rubble—burned as they are?"

Tobiah the Ammonite, who was at his side, said, "What they are building—even a fox climbing up on it would break down their wall of stones!"

Hear us, our God, for we are despised. Turn their insults back on their own heads. Give them over as plunder in a land of captivity. Do not cover up their guilt or blot out their sins from your sight, for they have thrown insults in the face of the builders.

So we rebuilt the wall till all of it reached half its height, for the people worked with all their heart.

But when Sanballat, Tobiah, the Arabs, the Ammonites and the people of Ashdod heard that the repairs to Jerusalem's walls had gone ahead and that the gaps were being closed, they were very angry. They all plotted together to come and fight against Jerusalem and stir up trouble against it. But we prayed to our God and posted a guard day and night to meet this threat.

Meanwhile, the people in Judah said, "The strength of the laborers is giving out, and there is so much rubble that we cannot rebuild the wall."

Also our enemies said, "Before they know it or see us, we will be right there among them and will kill them and put an end to the work."

Then the Jews who lived near them came and told us ten times over, "Wherever you turn, they will attack us."

Therefore I stationed some of the people behind the lowest points of the wall at the exposed places, posting them by families, with their swords, spears and bows. After I looked things over, I stood up and said to the nobles, the officials and the rest of the people, "Don't be afraid of them. Remember the Lord, who is great and awesome, and fight for your families, your sons and your daughters, your wives and your homes."

When our enemies heard that we were aware of their plot and that God had frustrated it, we all returned to the wall, each to our own work.

From that day on, half of my men did the work, while the other half were equipped with spears, shields, bows and armor. The officers posted themselves behind all the people of Judah who were building the wall. Those who carried materials did their work with one hand and held a weapon in the other, and each of the builders wore his sword at his side as he worked. But the man who sounded the trumpet stayed with me.

Then I said to the nobles, the officials and the rest of the people, "The work is extensive and spread out, and we are widely separated from each other along the wall. Wherever you hear the sound of the trumpet, join us there. Our God will fight for us!"

So we continued the work with half the men holding spears, from the first light of dawn till the stars came out. At that time I also said to the people, "Have every man and his helper stay inside Jerusalem at night, so they can serve us as guards by night and as workers by day." Neither I nor my brothers nor my men nor the guards with me took off our clothes; each had his weapon, even when he went for water.

When word came to Sanballat, Tobiah, Geshem the Arab and the rest of our enemies that I had rebuilt the wall and not a gap was left in it—though up to that time I had not set the doors in the gates—Sanballat and Geshem sent me this message: "Come, let us meet together in one of the villages on the plain of Ono."

But they were scheming to harm me; so I sent messengers to them with this reply: "I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down. Why should the work stop while I leave it and go down to you?" Four times they sent me the same message, and each time I gave them the same answer.

Then, the fifth time, Sanballat sent his aide to me with the same message, and in his hand was an unsealed letter in which was written: "It is reported among the nations—and Geshem says it is true—that you and the Jews are plotting to revolt, and therefore you are building the wall. Moreover, according to these reports you want to become their king and have even appointed prophets to make this proclamation about you in Jerusalem: 'There is a king in Judah!' Now this report will get back to the king; so come, let us meet together."

I sent him this reply: "Nothing like what you are saying is happening; you are just making it up out of your head."

They were all trying to frighten us, thinking, "Their hands will get too weak for the work, and it will not be completed." But I prayed, "Now strengthen my hands."

Discussion Questions

  • Before Nehemiah even laid a single brick, Sanballat and Tobiah were already mocking the work. Do you think opposition before you even begin is a common experience for men trying to build something meaningful?
  • Nehemiah's response to the mockery wasn't to argue or defend himself—it was simply, "So we built the wall." When do you think engaging with critics is the wrong move?
  • Nehemiah's men built with a tool in one hand and a weapon in the other. What do you think it looks like to build something while also staying guarded against real threats?
  • Nehemiah's enemies tried to get him to "come down" for a meeting—it seemed reasonable on the surface. Do you think distractions often disguise themselves as legitimate opportunities?
  • Nehemiah said, "I am doing a great work and I cannot come down." What would a man need to believe about what he's building to be able to say that?
