Week 9

Pre-Reading Context

Joseph — The eleventh of Jacob's twelve sons and the firstborn of Rachel, the wife Jacob loved most. His father favored him openly, giving him a robe of many colors that set him apart from his brothers. When Joseph was seventeen, God gave him two dreams showing his family bowing down to him. His brothers already hated him for their father's favoritism; the dreams pushed them over the edge. They stripped him of his robe, threw him into a pit, and sold him to traders for twenty shekels of silver—the price of a slave. They dipped his robe in goat's blood and let their father believe Joseph had been torn apart by a wild animal. Joseph was carried to Egypt in chains while his father mourned him as dead.

Potiphar — An Egyptian official of Pharaoh, captain of the guard. His title indicates he was head of Pharaoh's personal security force, possibly overseeing royal executions. He was a man of significant wealth and authority. When Joseph arrived in Egypt as a slave, Potiphar purchased him from the traders.

Potiphar's Wife — Scripture does not give her name. She repeatedly attempted to seduce Joseph, and when he refused, she falsely accused him of the very crime he had fled. Her lie sent Joseph from Potiphar's household to Pharaoh's prison.

The Chief Cupbearer — A high-ranking official in Pharaoh's court responsible for the king's wine. This was no mere servant role—the cupbearer was trusted with Pharaoh's life, ensuring his drink was safe from poison. He had direct access to the king daily. When he offended Pharaoh in some way, he was thrown into the same prison where Joseph was held.

The Chief Baker — Another trusted official responsible for Pharaoh's food. Like the cupbearer, he held a position of great responsibility and access to the king. He was imprisoned alongside the cupbearer after offending Pharaoh.

When Joseph arrived in Egypt, he had lost everything—his family, his home, his freedom, his future. He was a seventeen-year-old Hebrew slave in a foreign land where he knew no one and no one knew him. Yet Scripture repeats one phrase like a refrain: "The LORD was with Joseph."

Because the LORD was with him, Joseph prospered. Potiphar recognized that everything Joseph touched succeeded, and he put Joseph in charge of his entire household. For about eleven years, Joseph served faithfully in Potiphar's house—until Potiphar's wife noticed him.

Day after day she pressured Joseph to sleep with her. Day after day he refused, telling her he would not sin against God. When she finally grabbed him by his garment, Joseph fled, leaving the garment in her hand. She used it as evidence against him, claiming he had tried to assault her. Potiphar threw Joseph into prison.

The normal punishment for such a crime was death. That Joseph was only imprisoned suggests Potiphar may have doubted his wife's story. But for Joseph, prison meant more years of waiting—innocent, forgotten, with no way out.

Yet even in prison, the LORD was with Joseph. The keeper of the prison put him in charge of all the other prisoners. And then two of Pharaoh's most trusted officials arrived—the chief cupbearer and chief baker—thrown into the same prison after offending the king.

One night, both men had dreams that troubled them deeply. In Egypt, dreams were considered messages from the gods, and these men had no interpreter. Joseph saw their distress and asked what was wrong. When they explained, Joseph's response revealed where his hope still rested: "Do not interpretations belong to God? Please tell them to me."
