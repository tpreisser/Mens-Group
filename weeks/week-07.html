Week 7

Pre-Reading Context

Jonah — A prophet from Gath-hepher in the northern kingdom of Israel. He is mentioned in 2 Kings 14:25 as prophesying during the reign of Jeroboam II, placing him in the eighth century BC. God called Jonah to preach against Nineveh. Jonah refused and fled in the opposite direction—boarding a ship bound for Tarshish, as far west as he could go. God sent a great storm. The sailors cast lots, identified Jonah as the cause, and threw him overboard at his own request. God appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah. After three days and nights, Jonah prayed, and the fish vomited him onto dry land. God's call came a second time: "Arise, go to Nineveh." This time Jonah went.

The Ninevites — The people of Nineveh, capital city of the Assyrian Empire. The Assyrians were not merely Israel's enemies—they were the most brutal empire the ancient world had known. Assyrian kings boasted of their atrocities: impaling prisoners alive, skinning captives, stacking pyramids of severed heads outside conquered cities, and deporting entire populations. They would later destroy the northern kingdom of Israel and carry its people into exile. When Jonah arrived, Nineveh was a vast city—three days' journey across—filled with people who had committed unspeakable violence.

God — The LORD, the God of Israel, who is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love. He is the God who relents from disaster when people turn from their sin. In this book, God demonstrates that His compassion extends beyond the borders of Israel to all peoples—even to Israel's cruelest enemies. He is sovereign over the storm, the fish, the plant, the worm, and the wind. He pursues the reluctant prophet just as He pursues the wicked city.

Jonah walked through Nineveh proclaiming: "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!" The response was unprecedented. The entire city believed God. From the greatest to the least, they put on sackcloth and fasted. Even the king rose from his throne, removed his royal robes, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes. He issued a decree: let every person and animal fast, let everyone call out mightily to God, and let every person turn from his evil way and from the violence in his hands. "Who knows?" the king said. "God may turn and relent and turn from his fierce anger, so that we may not perish."

God saw their repentance. He saw that they turned from their evil way. And God relented of the disaster He had said He would bring upon them.

This should have been the greatest moment of Jonah's prophetic career. No Israelite prophet had ever seen such a response. Throughout Israel's history, prophets had called God's own people to repentance and been rejected, persecuted, even killed. Yet here, the most wicked city on earth had turned to God at Jonah's word.

But Jonah was not celebrating. He was furious.

This is where our passage begins—with Jonah's anger and God's response.

Reading

When God saw what they did and how they turned from their evil ways, he relented and did not bring on them the destruction he had threatened.

But to Jonah this seemed very wrong, and he became angry. He prayed to the LORD, "Isn't this what I said, LORD, when I was still at home? That is what I tried to forestall by fleeing to Tarshish. I knew that you are a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in love, a God who relents from sending calamity. Now, LORD, take away my life, for it is better for me to die than to live."

But the LORD replied, "Is it right for you to be angry?"

Jonah had gone out and sat down at a place east of the city. There he made himself a shelter, sat in its shade and waited to see what would happen to the city. Then the LORD God provided a leafy plant and made it grow up over Jonah to give shade for his head to ease his discomfort, and Jonah was very happy about the plant. But at dawn the next day God provided a worm, which chewed the plant so that it withered. When the sun rose, God provided a scorching east wind, and the sun blazed on Jonah's head so that he grew faint. He wanted to die, and said, "It would be better for me to die than to live."

But God said to Jonah, "Is it right for you to be angry about the plant?"

"It is," he said. "And I'm so angry I wish I were dead."

But the LORD said, "You have been concerned about this plant, though you did not tend it or make it grow. It sprang up overnight and died overnight. And should I not have concern for the great city of Nineveh, in which there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand people who cannot tell their right hand from their left—and also many animals?"

Discussion Questions

  • Jonah preached to Nineveh, they repented, and God showed them mercy. Instead of celebrating, Jonah was furious. Have you ever been frustrated when someone received grace that you didn't think they deserved?
  • God asked Jonah twice: "Do you do well to be angry?" It's a simple question, but a piercing one. Do you think most men honestly ask themselves that when they're heated?
  • Jonah was more upset about a plant that died than about 120,000 people who could have been destroyed. What do you think this reveals about how personal comfort can distort a man's priorities?
  • The book of Jonah ends with God's question hanging in the air—no answer, no resolution. Why do you think the story ends that way?
  • Jonah's anger about Nineveh revealed something deeper going on inside him. Do you think the things that set us off often point to something underneath the surface?
