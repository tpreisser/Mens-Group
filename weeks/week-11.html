Week 11

Pre-Reading Context

Solomon — The son of David, third king of Israel, and the man to whom God gave wisdom beyond any who came before or after him. When Solomon was young, God appeared to him in a dream and offered him anything he asked. Solomon asked for wisdom to lead God's people. Pleased with this request, God gave him not only unparalleled wisdom but also riches and honor beyond measure. Solomon built the temple in Jerusalem—the house of God that his father David had longed to build. Kings and queens traveled from distant lands to hear his wisdom. He composed thousands of proverbs and songs. Scripture says there was no king like Solomon in all the earth.

Yet Solomon did not finish well. He took seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, many of them foreign women who worshiped other gods. The LORD had explicitly warned Israel not to intermarry with such nations, "for surely they will turn away your heart after their gods." And that is exactly what happened. When Solomon was old, his wives turned his heart after Ashtoreth, goddess of the Sidonians, and Milcom, the abomination of the Ammonites. He built high places for these false gods on the hills around Jerusalem. Scripture delivers this verdict: "Solomon did what was evil in the sight of the LORD and did not wholly follow the LORD, as David his father had done."

The Preacher — This is the title Solomon uses for himself in Ecclesiastes. The Hebrew word is Qoheleth, meaning "one who addresses an assembly." It is the word from which we get the book's English title—Ecclesiastes comes from the Greek word for assembly. Solomon writes not as king giving decrees, but as a teacher sharing hard-won wisdom with those who will listen.

Ecclesiastes is believed to have been written late in Solomon's life, after he had pursued every earthly pleasure and accomplishment imaginable—and found them all wanting. The book opens with a phrase that will echo throughout: "Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity."

The Hebrew word translated "vanity" is hevel. It means breath, vapor, mist—something that appears for a moment and then vanishes. Solomon is not saying life is worthless. He is saying that life "under the sun"—life lived from a purely earthly perspective, life pursued apart from God—is like trying to grasp smoke. You close your hand around it, and there is nothing there.

The phrase "under the sun" appears twenty-nine times in Ecclesiastes. It describes the horizon of human existence when we look only at what we can see, touch, accomplish, and accumulate. From this vantage point, Solomon examines everything: wisdom, pleasure, labor, wealth, accomplishment. He had more of each than any man alive. And he declares them all hevel—fleeting, elusive, unable to satisfy.

This is not despair. This is a man who squandered extraordinary blessings trying to find fulfillment apart from the God who gave them, now warning others not to make the same mistake. The book will eventually conclude: "Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man."

The passage begins with Solomon's opening declaration about the nature of life under the sun.

Reading

The words of the Teacher, son of David, king in Jerusalem: "Meaningless! Meaningless!" says the Teacher. "Utterly meaningless! Everything is meaningless." What do people gain from all their labors at which they toil under the sun? Generations come and generations go, but the earth remains forever. The sun rises and the sun sets, and hurries back to where it rises. The wind blows to the south and turns to the north; round and round it goes, ever returning on its course. All streams flow into the sea, yet the sea is never full. To the place the streams come from, there they return again. All things are wearisome, more than one can say. The eye never has enough of seeing, nor the ear its fill of hearing. What has been will be again, what has been done will be done again; there is nothing new under the sun. Is there anything of which one can say, "Look! This is something new"? It was here already, long ago; it was here before our time. No one remembers the former generations, and even those yet to come will not be remembered by those who follow them.

I, the Teacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem. I applied my mind to study and to explore by wisdom all that is done under the heavens. What a heavy burden God has laid on mankind! I have seen all the things that are done under the sun; all of them are meaningless, a chasing after the wind. What is crooked cannot be straightened; what is lacking cannot be counted. I said to myself, "Look, I have increased in wisdom more than anyone who has ruled over Jerusalem before me; I have experienced much of wisdom and knowledge." Then I applied myself to the understanding of wisdom, and also of madness and folly, but I learned that this, too, is a chasing after the wind. For with much wisdom comes much sorrow; the more knowledge, the more grief.

I said to myself, "Come now, I will test you with pleasure to find out what is good." But that also proved to be meaningless. "Laughter," I said, "is madness. And what does pleasure accomplish?" I tried cheering myself with wine, and embracing folly—my mind still guiding me with wisdom. I wanted to see what was good for people to do under the heavens during the few days of their lives. I undertook great projects: I built houses for myself and planted vineyards. I made gardens and parks and planted all kinds of fruit trees in them. I made reservoirs to water groves of flourishing trees. I bought male and female slaves and had other slaves who were born in my house. I also owned more herds and flocks than anyone in Jerusalem before me. I amassed silver and gold for myself, and the treasure of kings and provinces. I acquired male and female singers, and a harem as well—the delights of a man's heart. I became greater by far than anyone in Jerusalem before me. In all this my wisdom stayed with me. I denied myself nothing my eyes desired; I refused my heart no pleasure. My heart took delight in all my labor, and this was the reward for all my toil. Yet when I surveyed all that my hands had done and what I had toiled to achieve, everything was meaningless, a chasing after the wind; nothing was gained under the sun.

Then I turned my thoughts to consider wisdom, and also madness and folly. What more can the king's successor do than what has already been done? I saw that wisdom is better than folly, just as light is better than darkness. The wise have eyes in their heads, while the fool walks in the darkness; but I came to realize that the same fate overtakes them both. Then I said to myself, "The fate of the fool will overtake me also. What then do I gain by being wise?" I said to myself, "This too is meaningless." For the wise, like the fool, will not be long remembered; the days have already come when both have been forgotten. Like the fool, the wise too must die! So I hated life, because the work that is done under the sun was grievous to me. All of it is meaningless, a chasing after the wind. I hated all the things I had toiled for under the sun, because I must leave them to the one who comes after me. And who knows whether that person will be wise or foolish? Yet they will have control over all the fruit of my toil into which I have poured my effort and skill under the sun. This too is meaningless. So my heart began to despair over all my toilsome labor under the sun. For a person may labor with wisdom, knowledge and skill, and then they must leave all they own to another who has not toiled for it. This too is meaningless and a great misfortune. What do people get for all the toil and anxious striving with which they labor under the sun? All their days their work is pain and grief; even at night their minds do not rest. This too is meaningless.

A person can do nothing better than to eat and drink and find satisfaction in their own toil. This too, I see, is from the hand of God, for without him, who can eat or find enjoyment? To the person who pleases him, God gives wisdom, knowledge and happiness, but to the sinner he gives the task of gathering and storing up wealth to hand it over to the one who pleases God. This too is meaningless, a chasing after the wind.

Discussion Questions

  • Solomon says "I denied myself nothing my eyes desired" -- and then says "I hated life." The guy who literally had everything still felt empty. What do you think that tells us about the thing we're all assuming is going to make us feel satisfied once we get it?
  • Have you ever hit a goal -- got the promotion, the relationship, the number in your account, whatever -- and instead of feeling fulfilled you just felt... nothing? What was that like?
  • Be honest: how much of what drives you on a daily basis is actually about comparing yourself to other men? Whether it's money, career, body, lifestyle -- how much of your grind is really just keeping score?
  • Solomon says he hated all his work because he'd have to leave it to somebody after him, and who knows if that person would be wise or a fool. Do you ever think about that -- that you can't control what happens to what you build after you're gone? Does that thought bother you or free you?
  • How often do you actually sit with the fact that your time here is limited? Or do most of us just shove that thought aside and stay busy? What do you think changes when a man actually lets that reality sink in?
  • There's this one line tucked in at the end -- "a person can do nothing better than to eat and drink and find satisfaction in their own toil. This too, I see, is from the hand of God." After everything Solomon tried, the answer was just... enjoying the simple stuff. Why do you think that's so hard for us to accept?
  • If somebody looked at how you actually spend your time and money -- not what you say matters, but where your hours and dollars actually go -- what would they say you believe is most important? Does that line up with what you want it to be?
