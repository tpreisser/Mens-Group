# Week 11 Pre-Read: The Man Who Had Everything
## Ecclesiastes 1:1-18, 2:1-26

---

### Characters

**Solomon** — The son of David, third king of Israel, and the man to whom God gave wisdom beyond any who came before or after him. When Solomon was young, God appeared to him in a dream and offered him anything he asked. Solomon asked for wisdom to lead God's people. Pleased with this request, God gave him not only unparalleled wisdom but also riches and honor beyond measure. Solomon built the temple in Jerusalem—the house of God that his father David had longed to build. Kings and queens traveled from distant lands to hear his wisdom. He composed thousands of proverbs and songs. Scripture says there was no king like Solomon in all the earth.

Yet Solomon did not finish well. He took seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines, many of them foreign women who worshiped other gods. The LORD had explicitly warned Israel not to intermarry with such nations, "for surely they will turn away your heart after their gods." And that is exactly what happened. When Solomon was old, his wives turned his heart after Ashtoreth, goddess of the Sidonians, and Milcom, the abomination of the Ammonites. He built high places for these false gods on the hills around Jerusalem. Scripture delivers this verdict: "Solomon did what was evil in the sight of the LORD and did not wholly follow the LORD, as David his father had done."

**The Preacher** — This is the title Solomon uses for himself in Ecclesiastes. The Hebrew word is *Qoheleth*, meaning "one who addresses an assembly." It is the word from which we get the book's English title—*Ecclesiastes* comes from the Greek word for assembly. Solomon writes not as king giving decrees, but as a teacher sharing hard-won wisdom with those who will listen.

---

### Context

Ecclesiastes is believed to have been written late in Solomon's life, after he had pursued every earthly pleasure and accomplishment imaginable—and found them all wanting. The book opens with a phrase that will echo throughout: "Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity."

The Hebrew word translated "vanity" is *hevel*. It means breath, vapor, mist—something that appears for a moment and then vanishes. Solomon is not saying life is worthless. He is saying that life "under the sun"—life lived from a purely earthly perspective, life pursued apart from God—is like trying to grasp smoke. You close your hand around it, and there is nothing there.

The phrase "under the sun" appears twenty-nine times in Ecclesiastes. It describes the horizon of human existence when we look only at what we can see, touch, accomplish, and accumulate. From this vantage point, Solomon examines everything: wisdom, pleasure, labor, wealth, accomplishment. He had more of each than any man alive. And he declares them all *hevel*—fleeting, elusive, unable to satisfy.

This is not despair. This is a man who squandered extraordinary blessings trying to find fulfillment apart from the God who gave them, now warning others not to make the same mistake. The book will eventually conclude: "Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man."

The passage begins with Solomon's opening declaration about the nature of life under the sun.
