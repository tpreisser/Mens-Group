# Week 4 Pre-Read: The One Who Stayed
## Luke 15:25-32

---

### Characters

**The Father** — A wealthy landowner with servants, livestock, and an estate large enough to divide between two sons. In this parable, he represents God. His actions would have shocked Jesus' original audience: granting a premature inheritance request, running through the village to embrace a returning disgrace, and throwing a lavish celebration for a son who had wished him dead. The father is the central figure—his grace is the point of the story.

**The Younger Son (The Prodigal)** — The son who demanded his inheritance early. In Jewish culture, this was equivalent to saying "I wish you were dead." He liquidated his share of the family estate, left for a distant country, and squandered everything on reckless living. When famine struck, he ended up feeding pigs—an unclean animal to Jews—and was so desperate he envied what the pigs ate. He returned home rehearsing a speech to ask for a position as a hired servant. The father's embrace interrupted him before he could finish. By the time our passage begins, he has been restored: given the father's best robe, a ring signifying authority, sandals marking him as family not servant, and a feast in his honor.

**The Older Son** — The one who stayed. He never left, never squandered anything, never brought public shame on the family. He was in the field working when his brother returned. He comes home to find music, dancing, and celebration—and learns it is all for his brother. His response reveals something about his heart that years of faithful service had concealed.

**The Tax Collectors and Sinners** — The crowd gathered around Jesus when he told this parable (Luke 15:1). They were the social and religious outcasts of Israel—people who had wandered far from God and knew it. They represent the younger son.

**The Pharisees and Scribes** — The religious leaders who triggered this parable by grumbling, "This man receives sinners and eats with them" (Luke 15:2). They were outwardly obedient, religiously devoted, and offended that Jesus welcomed obvious sinners. They represent the older son.

---

### Context

Jesus told this parable because the Pharisees were angry. Tax collectors and sinners kept drawing near to hear him teach, and Jesus not only welcomed them—he ate with them. Table fellowship meant acceptance, and the religious leaders could not understand why Jesus would accept people who had so clearly rejected God's ways.

So Jesus told three parables in a row: a shepherd who left ninety-nine sheep to find one that was lost, a woman who searched her whole house for one lost coin, and a father with two lost sons.

Most people call this the Parable of the Prodigal Son. But the younger son's story ends at verse 24. Everything from verse 25 onward is about the older brother—and Jesus never tells us how his story ends.

Here is what happened before our passage begins:

The younger son had taken his inheritance, converted it to cash, and left. In doing so he had publicly shamed his father and rejected everything his family stood for. He had gone to a Gentile country, lived in ways that violated the law of God, and ended up so degraded that he was willing to eat pig food. When he finally returned, he expected to beg for a position as a hired worker.

Instead, his father saw him from a distance and ran. In that culture, dignified men did not run—it required hiking up your robe and exposing your legs. But the father ran anyway, embraced his filthy son before the village could reject him, and began shouting orders: bring the best robe, put a ring on his hand, get sandals for his feet, kill the fattened calf. There would be no slow process of earning his way back. There would be a party.

The older son was not there for any of this. He was in the field, doing what he had always done—working. When he came home and heard the music, he had to ask a servant what was happening. The servant told him: "Your brother has come, and your father has killed the fattened calf, because he has received him back safe and sound."

The older brother's reaction was immediate. He became angry and refused to go in.
